PixiJS DotFilter Halftone Comic Effect Explained

PixiJS enables developers to convert smooth, continuous-tone digital graphics into vintage halftone comic book prints using the DotFilter. This article explains the underlying mechanism of the filter, covering how it subdivides textures into coordinate grids, samples luminance to scale geometric dots, and uses GLSL fragment shaders to recreate traditional retro printmaking on the GPU in real time.

Grid Subdivision in Screen Space

The transformation begins in the WebGL fragment shader by dividing the sprite’s continuous UV coordinate space into a repeating matrix of square cells. Controlled by the filter’s scale property, the shader quantizes incoming pixel coordinates. Instead of treating each pixel as an isolated fragment with its own texture lookup, the shader groups neighboring pixels into distinct discrete tiles. This grid serves as the foundational lattice where individual halftone dots will reside.

Angle Transformation and Screen Angles

In traditional offset and CMYK printing, printing plates are rotated at distinct angles to prevent visual interference known as moiré patterns. The DotFilter replicates this through an angle parameter. Before determining cell boundaries, the shader applies a 2D rotation matrix to the UV coordinates:

\[\begin{bmatrix} x' \\ y' \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} \cos(\theta) & -\sin(\theta) \\ \sin(\theta) & \cos(\theta) \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} x \\ y \end{bmatrix}\]

Rotating the coordinate space before snapping fragments to the nearest grid center allows the dot pattern to tilt diagonally—typically at 45 or 15 degrees—mimicking vintage comic printing presses.

Luminance Evaluation

For each cell in the rotated grid, the shader identifies a single anchor point, usually the exact center. It samples the original sprite texture at this center coordinate to determine the overall brightness (luminance) of that specific block.

Luminance is calculated using the standard perceptual color weights:

\[\text{Luminance} = 0.299R + 0.587G + 0.114B\]

This produces a single scalar value between \(0.0\) and \(1.0\). In light areas of the image, the luminance value approaches \(1.0\), while in shadow or dark areas, it drops toward \(0.0\).

Dynamic Radius and Distance Fields

Once luminance is determined, the filter computes the radius of the dot to be drawn inside that specific cell. The dot size is proportional to the brightness: darker areas yield smaller dots (or larger dots if rendering ink-on-paper where ink coverage represents darkness), while lighter areas scale inversely.

The shader then evaluates each pixel inside the cell by calculating its Euclidean distance from the cell center. Using signed distance field (SDF) mathematics or the GLSL step and smoothstep functions:

  • If the fragment's distance to the center is less than the calculated radius, the fragment is classified as inside the dot.
  • If the distance is greater, it is marked as empty space (the background).

Using smoothstep adds subtle anti-aliasing along the boundary of each dot, eliminating harsh, pixelated edges and producing clean, round vector-like points.

Output Compositing

Finally, the shader assigns colors based on the distance test. Fragments inside the calculated radius display either the sampled color of the sprite or a defined foreground ink color, while fragments outside the radius receive a transparent or solid paper background color. Because this entire mathematical process executes in parallel across millions of pixels on the GPU, the DotFilter transforms complex animations, video feeds, and interactive sprites into dynamic halftone illustrations with negligible performance overhead.
