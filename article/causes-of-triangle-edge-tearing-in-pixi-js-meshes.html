Causes of Triangle Edge Tearing in Pixi.js Meshes

Visual tearing, seams, and edge artifacts in deformed Pixi.js meshes typically stem from a combination of GPU rasterization limitations, floating-point rounding errors, and texture filtering mechanics. When meshes undergo dynamic transformations—such as skeletal rigging, skinning, or vertex displacement—the shared boundaries between adjacent triangles can expose microscopic gaps or sample unwanted texels. Understanding the technical behavior of WebGL rendering pipelines, UV mapping precision, and texture atlas boundaries makes diagnosing and resolving these visual anomalies straightforward.

Sub-Pixel Rasterization Gaps

Modern GPUs use conservative rasterization rules to determine which pixels fall inside a triangle. When vertices are deformed into fractional (sub-pixel) floating-point coordinates, slight precision divergences can occur along the shared edge between two adjacent triangles. Even though two triangles share the exact same vertex positions in memory, the rasterizer calculates pixel coverage per triangle independently.

If the calculated edge does not align perfectly with the pixel grid, fragments along the seam may fail the coverage test for both triangles. This creates visible one-pixel "cracks" or hairline gaps that reveal the background color behind the mesh.

Bilinear Texture Filtering and Gutter Bleeding

Pixi.js applies bilinear interpolation by default to smooth textures when scaled or stretched. Bilinear filtering samples a 2x2 grid of texels surrounding the target UV coordinate.

When a mesh deforms, triangle UVs are stretched non-uniformly. If UV coordinates sit too close to the boundary of a sprite inside a texture atlas, bilinear filtering samples pixels from outside the intended area. If adjacent space in the atlas is transparent black (rgba(0,0,0,0)), this results in dark fringes or transparent tears along the outer and inner triangle edges.

Affine Texture Mapping Seams

In 2D WebGL engines like Pixi.js, quads are typically constructed from two triangles sharing a diagonal edge. WebGL utilizes affine texture mapping across flat 2D triangles.

When a quad is deformed irregularly (for instance, moving a single corner vertex while leaving others fixed), the two triangles stretch differently. Because affine mapping interpolates coordinates linearly across the surface of each individual triangle, the UV rate of change abruptly shifts across the shared diagonal. This discrepancy manifests as a visible distortion seam or visual "crease" running directly through the deformed mesh.

Shared Vertex Disconnects and Precision Drift

Tearing frequently occurs when adjacent triangles do not actually share identical vertex references in memory. If a mesh is constructed with duplicated vertices for adjacent polygons rather than an indexed triangle list, custom deformation logic applied via code or shaders might compute slight positional differences between those "shared" points. Even an offset as small as 0.0001 pixels can break edge continuity, resulting in obvious background tearing under movement.

Solutions and Mitigations

  • Texture Padding and Extrusion: Add at least 1–2 pixels of color padding (extrusion) around sprite borders within texture atlases to prevent transparent or adjacent pixels from bleeding during bilinear sampling.
  • Indexed Meshes: Always use indexed geometry (PIXI.MeshGeometry with an index buffer) so shared triangle edges draw from identical vertex coordinates.
  • Canvas Multisampling (MSAA): Enable antialiasing on the Pixi.js Application (antialias: true) to smooth sub-pixel rasterization gaps along shared polygon boundaries.
  • Tessellation Density: Subdivide large, heavily deformed quads into denser triangle grids. Higher polygon density reduces the extreme distortion across any single triangle, smoothing out affine mapping artifacts.
