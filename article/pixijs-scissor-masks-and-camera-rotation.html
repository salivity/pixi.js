PixiJS Scissor Masks and Camera Rotation

In PixiJS, scissor masks offer a high-performance solution for rectangular clipping by leveraging native WebGL scissor testing. However, because the underlying hardware scissor test is strictly bound to screen-space coordinates, camera or container rotation breaks the expected alignment between the mask and the rendered content. This article explains the technical mechanics behind scissor mask limitations during rotation, how it affects rendering accuracy, and how to properly handle rotated masks in your application.

The Axis-Aligned Nature of WebGL Scissor Tests

Scissor masking in PixiJS maps directly to WebGL’s gl.scissor() API. This hardware feature restricts rendering to a specific rectangular sub-region of the canvas. The fundamental constraint of gl.scissor() is that it is strictly axis-aligned to the viewport (the physical display or canvas pixels). It accepts only four parameters: an x-coordinate, a y-coordinate, a width, and a height. Because it operates at the rasterizer level before pixel shading, it contains no mathematical mechanism to apply a transformation matrix or handle rotation angles.

How Camera Rotation Disrupts Scissor Masks

When a camera rotates in PixiJS—typically achieved by rotating a root container or a viewport plugin—the transformation matrices of the children within that camera update accordingly. When a scissor mask is evaluated under a rotated transform, PixiJS must project the local rectangular bounds into screen space. Because WebGL cannot rotate the scissor rectangle, one of two adverse effects occurs:

  1. Bounding Box Inflation (AABB): If PixiJS calculates an Axis-Aligned Bounding Box (AABB) to encompass the rotated rectangle, the clipping region expands. As a rectangle rotates 45 degrees, its screen-space AABB grows significantly larger than the intended mask area, exposing content that should remain hidden at the corners.
  2. Misalignment with Visual Geometry: If the scissor region remains tied to screen coordinates without adapting to the camera's rotational matrix, the clipped content rotates inside a stationary, upright window. The content appears to tilt, but the edge of the cut remains locked horizontally and vertically relative to the screen.

In both scenarios, the visual relationship between the masking boundary and the masked object is lost. The mask fails to rotate alongside the target geometry.

Scissor Masks vs. Stencil Masks

The primary reason developers choose scissor masks is performance. Scissor tests discard fragments before expensive operations occur, avoiding the overhead of clearing and writing to extra GPU buffers.

However, when arbitrary transformation—such as rotation, scaling on skewed axes, or perspective distortion—is required, scissor masks are technically incapable of delivering correct results. Stencil masks or texture-based masks must be used instead:

  • Stencil Masks: By assigning a PIXI.Graphics object as a mask (displayObject.mask = graphics), PixiJS uses the WebGL stencil buffer. Stencil masks undergo standard vertex transformation passes, meaning they rotate, scale, and translate seamlessly with camera movement. This incurs a small performance cost due to stencil buffer writes and state changes, but it ensures pixel-perfect geometric clipping.
  • Alpha/Sprite Masks: If you need smooth or anti-aliased edges alongside rotation, a sprite mask paired with a custom filter handles arbitrary angles by sampling a mask texture in a fragment shader.

Best Practices for Rotated Scenes

If your PixiJS application involves a dynamic camera that rotates, zoom-pans, or tilts, avoid using scissor masks on any elements that reside inside the rotated coordinate space. Reserve scissor masks exclusively for static, screen-locked UI layers that remain parallel to the canvas borders (such as fixed HUDs or minimaps). For any in-world elements subject to camera rotation, use stencil-based graphics masks to maintain visual consistency across all view angles.
