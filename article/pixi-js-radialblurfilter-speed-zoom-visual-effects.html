Pixi.js RadialBlurFilter Speed Zoom Visual Effects

The RadialBlurFilter in Pixi.js generates dynamic speed zoom effects by blurring an object's pixels along rays projecting outward from a defined center point. This article explains the underlying shader mechanics of radial blurring, breaks down the core parameters that govern the intensity and direction of the zoom, and details how the GPU samples textures to produce the illusion of high-velocity camera motion.

The Underlying Mechanics: Directional Ray Sampling

Unlike a standard Gaussian blur that distributes samples evenly in a 2D radius around a pixel, a radial blur relies on vector math directed relative to a single focal origin.

In WebGL, the RadialBlurFilter runs a custom fragment shader over every pixel of the target display object:

  1. Vector Calculation: The shader computes a directional vector for each fragment by subtracting the effect's focal center coordinates from the current fragment's texture coordinates (uv - center).
  2. Radial Stepping: The fragment shader iterates through a predefined loop of texture samples. In each step of the loop, it shifts the sampling coordinates along the directional vector toward or away from the center point.
  3. Accumulation and Averaging: The color values from each sample along this ray are accumulated and divided by the total number of iterations.

Because the directional vector grows longer as pixels move farther from the focal center, peripheral pixels experience a much larger sampling offset than pixels near the center. This differential displacement creates the optical streaking that human perception associates with rapid forward or backward acceleration.

Key Parameters Controlling the Effect

Achieving a convincing speed zoom requires fine-tuning specific properties exposed by the filter:

  • center ([x, y]): Defines the epicenter of the zoom in pixel coordinates. For a classic forward-acceleration effect, this is typically set to the center of the viewport or aligned to an active sprite, such as the nose of a moving vehicle or a player character.
  • radius: Determines the distance from the center where the blur begins. Setting a non-zero radius leaves a clear, unblurred "eye" around the focal point, ensuring critical visual information remains sharp while the surrounding scene streaks outward.
  • angle / strength: Controls the magnitude of the displacement along the rays. Higher values increase the distance between sample points, resulting in longer visual streaks that convey greater speed.
  • kernelSize: The number of texture lookups performed per fragment. Higher values yield smooth, cinematic streaks without noticeable banding, but increase GPU overhead.

Simulating Dynamic Acceleration

A static radial blur merely looks like a blurred still image. To simulate dynamic speed zoom, developers animate the filter's properties across frames:

  • Ramping Strength: Rapidly interpolating the blur strength from 0 to a peak value creates the visual impact of an instantaneous speed burst, such as activating a warp drive or executing a dash move.
  • Tracking Objects: Continuously updating the center property to match a moving sprite's global position keeps the focal point anchored to the moving entity, simulating a camera locked onto a high-speed target.
  • Focal Pumping: Oscillating the blur intensity alongside a slight scale increase of the target container enhances the physical sensation of rapid forward momentum.

Performance Considerations

Because the RadialBlurFilter relies on multiple dependent texture reads within a loop for every rendered pixel, it is fill-rate intensive. On mobile devices or integrated GPUs, excessive kernelSize values can lead to frame drops. To maintain high performance, developers typically use lower sample counts combined with slight overall scene motion blur, or apply the filter to a downscaled render texture before compositing it onto the main stage.
