PixiJS Filter vs Material: Key Differences

In PixiJS, both Filter programs and Material programs utilize custom WebGL/WebGPU shaders to render visual effects, but they serve fundamentally different purposes within the rendering pipeline. A Filter program is a post-processing tool applied to any DisplayObject (such as Sprites or Containers) by rendering it to an off-screen texture before applying shader effects. In contrast, a Material program (used primarily with Mesh objects) is applied directly during the geometry rendering phase, dictating how vertices and pixels are drawn in real-time without an intermediate off-screen buffer. Understanding the distinction between these two approaches is essential for optimizing rendering performance and achieving precise visual results.

What is a Filter Program?

A Filter program in PixiJS is designed for post-processing. When you assign a filter to a display object:

  1. Off-Screen Render Pass: PixiJS pauses the standard render pass, creates an off-screen framebuffer (render texture), and draws the target object and all of its nested children onto that texture.
  2. Shader Application: The filter’s fragment shader is executed on the resulting texture.
  3. Composite Output: The processed texture is drawn back onto the main canvas or destination buffer.

Because filters capture the bounding area of an object and its children, they operate in screen space or texture coordinate space (using uniforms like uSampler, filterArea, and filterClamp). Filters are ideal for global effects such as Gaussian blurs, color matrix adjustments, bloom, displacement maps, and drop shadows.

What is a Material Program?

A Material program (often implemented via MeshMaterial or custom Shader instances) defines the visual properties of a specific piece of geometry. When used with a Mesh:

  1. Direct Geometry Rendering: The material’s vertex shader processes the mesh's raw vertex positions, and the fragment shader computes the colors directly onto the current render target.
  2. No Extra Framebuffers: There is no intermediate off-screen rendering pass required, eliminating the overhead of switching render targets.
  3. Local Vertex Control: The shader has direct access to geometry attributes such as custom UV coordinates, vertex colors, and vertex positions.

Materials are ideal for procedural terrain, custom 2D/3D geometry, flag-waving simulations, custom lighting, and deformations where vertex manipulation or direct fragment output is needed.

Key Differences

1. Rendering Pipeline and Performance

  • Filters: Rely on secondary render passes and framebuffer switches. Stacking multiple filters or applying filters to many individual objects can cause performance bottlenecks due to GPU context switching and memory allocation.
  • Materials: Integrate directly into the standard draw call pipeline. While custom materials may break automatic sprite batching, they do not require extra render textures or frame buffer switches, making them generally faster for geometry-specific effects.

2. Target Application

  • Filters: Can be assigned to almost any DisplayObject, including Sprite, Container, and Graphics. A single filter can alter an entire hierarchy of sprites simultaneously.
  • Materials: Bind exclusively to geometry-based objects, primarily Mesh and its derivatives (such as SimpleMesh or Rope). They cannot be applied directly across a generic container of mixed display elements.

3. Coordinate Spaces and Uniforms

  • Filters: Use normalized texture coordinates mapped to the bounding box of the filtered region. PixiJS automatically injects uniforms that handle texture clamping and screen-space mapping to prevent sampling artifacts outside the object's bounds.
  • Materials: Rely on geometry-level attributes. You work directly with model-view-projection matrices, vertex-defined UV coordinates, and custom vertex attributes.

4. Vertex Manipulation

  • Filters: Primarily focus on pixel manipulation in the fragment shader. While a filter can alter vertex positions via its vertex shader, it only operates on a full-screen quad representing the generated texture.
  • Materials: Allow full manipulation of the underlying mesh geometry. The vertex shader can translate, scale, or distort individual vertices prior to rasterization.

Summary

Use a Filter program when you need post-processing effects that alter an entire object or container after it has been drawn, such as color grading, blurring, or screen-space distortion. Use a Material program when you need direct control over how specific geometry is drawn, require vertex manipulation, or want to avoid the performance cost of off-screen framebuffers.
