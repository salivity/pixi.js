PixiJS Precision Jitter at Large Coordinates

Floating-point precision jitter occurs in PixiJS when rendering objects at vast distances from the world origin due to the limitations of 32-bit floating-point math in WebGL shaders. PixiJS processes scene graphs using 64-bit numbers on the CPU, but it flattens and passes these values to the GPU as 32-bit floats, which quickly lose sub-pixel precision beyond certain coordinate thresholds. Because PixiJS does not feature an automated, built-in double-precision rendering pipeline, resolving this jitter relies on architectural techniques within the engine, most notably the floating origin pattern and chunk-based local coordinate spaces.

The Underlying Cause of Jitter

JavaScript natively represents numbers using IEEE 754 double-precision (64-bit) floats, allowing for over 15 significant decimal digits. This ensures that CPU-side physics, movement logic, and transform hierarchies remain mathematically accurate even millions of units away from the origin (0, 0).

However, the WebGL pipeline standardizes on single-precision (32-bit) floats for vertex positions and transformation matrices. A 32-bit float provides only about 7 significant decimal digits. When world coordinates reach high values (typically around 65,536 units and worsening significantly past a million), the gap between consecutive representable numbers widens beyond a single pixel. When vertices are transformed inside the vertex shader, truncation occurs, manifesting visually as stuttering, vertex tearing, or "jitter" during smooth movement.

How PixiJS Handles the Transformation Pipeline

PixiJS does not automatically convert distant coordinates into camera-relative local offsets in its standard batching pipeline. Its transform pipeline functions as follows:

  1. CPU Transform Calculation: PixiJS computes a node's worldTransform by traversing the container tree. These matrix calculations use native 64-bit floats.
  2. Batch Upload: When rendering, PixiJS generates vertex data or updates uniform matrices. For standard 2D meshes, sprites, and graphics, positions are multiplied by the projection and world transformation matrices.
  3. GPU Execution: Values are packed into standard Float32Array buffers. Once loaded onto the GPU, the fractional accuracy required to place a sprite at, for example, 1,000,000.25 is lost, rounding the position to 1,000,000.0.

Because the default PixiJS batch renderer prioritizes speed and low draw calls over double-precision emulation, the engine delegates precision management to how the developer constructs the scene graph.

Architectural Solutions in PixiJS

To prevent jitter at scale, developers must keep the coordinates processed by the GPU as small as possible.

1. The Floating Origin Pattern (Camera-Centric Rendering)

The standard solution in PixiJS is to invert how the camera is moved. Instead of placing objects at their absolute world coordinates and moving a virtual camera container into the millions, the camera is anchored near (0, 0).

  • The player or viewport center remains permanently near the coordinate origin.
  • A master world Container is translated in the opposite direction of the camera movement.
  • When the camera moves to (1,000,000, 0), the world container is moved to (-1,000,000, 0). While this shifts large coordinates to the master container, local children still inherit large world coordinates if unmanaged.
  • To fully resolve GPU precision loss, the absolute coordinate simulation is decoupled from the PixiJS display hierarchy: entities maintain true 64-bit positions in pure data, and their PixiJS x and y properties are updated strictly relative to the camera viewport (screenPosition = worldPosition - cameraPosition). This guarantees that GPU coordinates never exceed the viewport's width and height.

2. Chunking and Local Coordinate Hierarchies

For expansive open worlds or tile-based maps, PixiJS applications typically segment the world into discrete spatial chunks:

  • The world is divided into grid cells (e.g., 1024x1024 units).
  • Each chunk is represented by a Container.
  • Objects inside the chunk have coordinates relative to the chunk's local origin (0 to 1024), preserving high precision.
  • As chunks enter the viewport, only visible chunks are offset relative to the camera, minimizing the magnitude of matrix values submitted to the GPU.

3. High-Precision Custom Shaders

For specialized applications requiring absolute large-scale coordinates on custom meshes without shifting the scene graph, developers implement "double-single" (DSFUN90) precision emulation in custom GLSL shaders.

In this approach:

  • The 64-bit coordinate is split on the CPU into two 32-bit floats: a high part (x_high) and a low part (x_low).
  • Both values are passed as separate attributes or uniforms to a custom PixiJS Filter or MeshMaterial.
  • The vertex shader reassembles the precision mathematically during matrix multiplication before projecting to clip space, eliminating truncation artifacts.
