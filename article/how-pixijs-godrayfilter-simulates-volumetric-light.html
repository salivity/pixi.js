How PixiJS GodrayFilter Simulates Volumetric Light

This article explains the underlying rendering techniques used by the GodrayFilter in PixiJS to generate dynamic volumetric lighting effects, commonly known as crepuscular rays. By breaking down its screen-space shader mechanics, texture sampling passes, and noise modulation, you will understand how a purely 2D post-processing pipeline efficiently simulates atmospheric light shafts without requiring complex 3D ray-marching.

Screen-Space Light Approximation

The GodrayFilter (available via the pixi-filters package) does not calculate true physical atmospheric scattering in a 3D coordinate space. Instead, it operates as a post-processing fragment shader on a rendered 2D texture.

The filter relies on screen-space radial blur. It assumes that bright pixels in the source display object act as light emitters, while surrounding darker pixels act as occluders. The shader smears these bright pixel values outward along vectors pointing away from a user-defined light source position.

Radial Directional Blur and Sampling

When rendering a specific fragment (pixel), the GPU executes the following sequence:

  1. Direction Vector Calculation: The shader computes a normalized 2D direction vector from the designated origin coordinate (center) to the current fragment coordinate.
  2. Ray Traversal (Accumulation): The shader samples the input texture multiple times along this vector toward the light center.
  3. Attenuation and Decay: Each sample is multiplied by an exponential decay factor. Pixels closest to the original sample retain higher intensity, while samples farther away lose energy. This replicates the visual attenuation of light traveling through a medium.
  4. Weighted Blend: The accumulated samples are summed together, scaled by an intensity (or gain) factor, and blended over the original scene texture.

Because bright areas cast their color values along the sampling trajectory over darker areas, visual shafts of light appear to stretch across the canvas.

Noise Modulation for Distinct Rays

A basic radial blur produces a smooth, uniform gradient around a bright object. To create the appearance of distinct, organic "god rays" breaking through a canopy or clouds, the GodrayFilter modulates the radial blur using procedural noise or coordinate distortion.

The fragment shader offsets the ray sampling weights using an internal mathematical noise algorithm or a trigonometric function driven by the pixel's angle relative to the center point. By exposing a time uniform, the shader animates this noise over time, giving the light shafts the appearance of shimmering atmospheric dust or moving cloud shadows.

Primary Filter Parameters

The look and performance of the filter depend on several uniform inputs:

  • center: The 2D coordinates [x, y] representing the focal point from which the rays emanate. Moving this point dynamically alters the ray angles across the entire scene.
  • gain: Controls the exposure or brightness of the accumulated light rays.
  • lacunarity: Influences the frequency and detail of the procedural ray striations.
  • time: Advances the noise phase to animate the rays frame-by-frame.
  • parallel: An optional boolean toggle in certain implementations that shifts the ray behavior from radial (emanating from a central point) to directional (parallel shafts falling at a fixed angle, simulating distant sunlight).

Performance Considerations

Because the GodrayFilter relies on multiple dependent texture reads per fragment inside a loop, it is computationally intensive relative to simple color-correction filters. PixiJS keeps this performant by clamping sample counts and executing the calculations directly on the GPU within a single post-processing pass. Lowering the resolution of the filter or minimizing the filter's target area helps maintain high frame rates on lower-end mobile devices.
